# Behavioral Interviews

Almost every onsite loop ends with a behavioral round, and candidates lose
offers there more often than they expect: it is the round people prepare least
and it is judged as closely as the coding rounds. It is also the cheapest
round to prepare for, at roughly three hours of work. This page covers the
method: how the round works, the STAR structure, your self-introduction, the
recurring question categories, and what to ask at the end.

## What the round is

Thirty to sixty minutes with your prospective manager, a teammate, or a
cross-functional interviewer. Instead of "solve this", you get "tell me about a
time when...". The interviewer is assessing how you work with people: how you
handle conflict, failure, ambiguity, and ownership, and how clearly you can
describe all of it. Answers draw exclusively on your real past projects, so
the preparation is about surfacing and structuring what you have already done.

## Answer with STAR

STAR is the standard structure for behavioral answers:

| Part | What it covers | Rough share |
|------|----------------|-------------|
| **S**ituation | The project and context, one or two sentences | 10% |
| **T**ask | What you specifically were responsible for | 10% |
| **A**ction | What you did, decided, or changed, step by step | 70% |
| **R**esult | The outcome, ideally with a number, and what you took away | 10% |

The weight sits on Action. Most weak answers spend too long on situation and
too little on what the candidate personally did. Keep the whole answer near
two minutes, use "I" rather than "we" for your actions, and finish with the
result even when the interviewer nods: an answer without a result is a story
without an ending.

## Prepare your self-introduction

The loop almost always opens with "tell me about yourself", and it sets the
interviewer's expectations for every round after it. Prepare about 90 seconds:

1. What you work on now, in one sentence.
2. One or two things you are proud of, with a concrete outcome each.
3. Why this role or company, in one sentence.

Rehearse it aloud until it sounds like you talking, not a document being
recited. It is the only answer you will give in every interview you ever take;
the rehearsal cost is tiny.

## The recurring question categories

Behavioral question banks run to hundreds of items, but they collapse into a
handful of categories:

| Category | Typical form |
|----------|--------------|
| Teamwork | Tell me about a time you worked closely on a team |
| Conflict | Describe a disagreement with a coworker or manager and how you resolved it |
| Failure | Tell me about your biggest mistake or a project that failed |
| Leadership | Tell me about a time you led a project or stepped beyond your role |
| Ambiguity | Tell me about a time you had to act without complete information |
| Pressure | Tell me about a time you delivered under a tight deadline or shifting requirements |
| Growth | Tell me about feedback that was hard to hear and what you did with it |

Prepare five to seven stories from real work, and write each one down in STAR
form. Choose stories that flex: a good one answers two or three categories
(the hard deadline is also the leadership story). Reusing a story across
categories is expected; every interviewer in a loop hearing the exact same
story is not, so know which of your stories are interchangeable.

## Questions to ask at the end

"Think of a time you..." ends with "what do you want to ask us?" Have three to
five real questions ready; "no, I'm good" reads as disinterest. Some safe,
substantive ones:

- What does a normal week look like for the team I would join?
- How are technical decisions made here, and who makes them?
- What would you want to see from me in the first six months?
- What is the hardest part of this role?
- Why did you join, and what has kept you?

Save compensation and logistics questions for the recruiter. In the room, ask
about the work and the people.

## How to practice

1. Write your self-introduction and your STAR stories. That is most of the
   three-hour budget.
2. Rehearse aloud, never in your head. Spoken answers feel different from
   written ones, and the difference is the point.
3. Fold behavioral rounds into your [mock interviews](mock_interviews.md):
   after the coding mock, spend ten minutes on two or three "tell me about a
   time" questions and ask the partner to interrupt and probe, the way real
   interviewers do.
